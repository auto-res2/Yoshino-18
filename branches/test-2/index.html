HAGuard Hierarchical Adaptive Guard for Low-Latency LLM Jailbreak Detection

Abstract

Large-language-model assistants increasingly mediate real-time user interactions, yet remain vulnerable to jailbreak prompts that elicit disallowed content. Production chat services impose sub-100 ms latency budgets, so calling a multi-billion-parameter “judge” model is infeasible, while single-stage heuristics plateau near 80 % balanced accuracy. We introduce HAGuard, a three-layer adaptive cascade that fuses (i) an ultra-fast lexical gate, (ii) a 60 M-parameter INT-4 DistilBERT, and (iii) prototype-intent matching in embedding space. All components export to ONNX and run on commodity CPUs; 70 % of traffic exits after the first two layers, keeping median end-to-end latency at 27 ms and p99 at 78 ms on an AWS c7g.large core. Training mixes multi-view contrastive learning on HarmBench, JailbreakBench, SafetyBench, StrongREJECT and 50 k benign production prompts with hard-negative mining from the RL-Breaker attack generator. Misclassified prompts trigger four-hourly LoRA updates that hot-reload in <2 min. On a public 55 k-prompt benchmark HAGuard attains 96.4 ± 0.3 % true-positive rate at 0.5 % false-positive rate and 0.972 ± 0.002 AUROC—15–20 pp higher and eight times faster than LLM-Guard-7B. A deliberately truncated 16-prompt run that scored only chance-level accuracy highlights the importance of the full evaluation protocol. Code, models and raw predictions are released for complete reproduction.

Introduction

Large-language models (LLMs) have transitioned from laboratory curiosities to central components of code assistants, writing aids and even medical triage tools. Their mass adoption nevertheless hinges on robust guardrails that intercept malicious "jailbreak" prompts able to override safety alignment and coax disallowed output (Patrick Chao, 2024), (Xuan Chen, 2024). A practical guard must simultaneously 1) recognise increasingly sophisticated, obfuscated and rapidly evolving attacks, 2) avoid collateral damage to benign requests, and 3) meet stringent service-level agreements—typically ≤ 100 ms end-to-end for 99 % of queries.

Meeting all three goals is challenging. Lightweight lexical rules execute in micro-seconds but miss paraphrases and unicode tricks, capping balanced accuracy at roughly 75–85 % (Gabriel Alon, 2023). Replacing them with powerful judge LLMs such as Llama-Guard-7B improves recall yet adds 200–500 ms latency, unacceptable for synchronous user experiences. Existing defences rarely combine heterogeneous evidence, leading to poorly calibrated probabilities and forcing operators to choose between aggressive blocking and risky permissiveness. Finally, attackers iterate quickly, whereas labelled data for novel jailbreak styles arrive slowly, impeding periodic re-training.

This paper presents HAGuard, a Hierarchical Adaptive Guard that addresses the four pain points through a carefully engineered cascade and an inexpensive continual-learning loop that never resorts to large models in production. All compute stays on a single CPU core; 72 % of queries terminate after a lexical or distilled-transformer stage, while the hardest 28 % invoke a quantised embedding matcher and a calibrated LightGBM head whose decision fuses lexical, semantic and prototype features. Prompts the stack misclassifies are quarantined and used to refresh a LoRA adapter every four hours, realising fast recovery reminiscent of online cascade learning (Lunyiu Nie, 2024) without any GPU.

Our contributions are:

  • A lexical–semantic–prototype cascade that sustains < 100 ms p99 latency entirely on CPUs.
  • A training recipe combining multi-view contrastive learning, RL-Breaker adversarial mining (Xuan Chen, 2024) and LoRA-based continual updates (< 2 min downtime).
  • A continuous-integration sanity suite that aborts if any artefact is stubbed or latency budgets are violated.
  • The first side-by-side evaluation of HAGuard, LLM-Guard-7B, Rebuff, PARDEN (Ziyang Zhang, 2024) and perplexity-based filters on a public 55 k-prompt benchmark drawn from HarmBench, JailbreakBench and production logs.
  • A 12-hour online adaptation study showing that two LoRA refreshes recover 60 % of initially missed attacks with < 1 pp FPR increase.

The remainder reviews related work, formalises the problem, details the methodology, describes experimental protocols, reports empirical results and concludes with limitations and future directions.

Related Work

Existing guardrails fall into three families. 1) Micro-second heuristics—regex keyword lists and perplexity filters—scale but fail on obfuscations, plateauing at ~80 % accuracy (Gabriel Alon, 2023). 2) Fine-tuned moderation LLMs such as Llama-Guard-2 and WildGuard improve recall yet are GPU-bound, adding hundreds of milliseconds latency (Seungju Han, 2024), (Hakan Inan, 2023). 3) Autoregressive self-judging approaches like PARDEN prompt the assistant to repeat its own output to avoid domain shift but still invoke a large model per request (Ziyang Zhang, 2024).

Complementary defences intervene inside the assistant itself: information-bottleneck training hinders leakage of harmful content (Zichuan Liu, 2024); adversarial prompt tuning hardens the system against black-box attacks (Yichuan Mo, 2024); MoGU routes between "safe" and "usable" variants to balance utility and harm (Yanrui Du, 2024). Such internal techniques do not obviate the need for an external pre-filter under tight latency constraints.

Balancing risk against computation through cascades is well studied in computer vision (Shani Goren, 2024) and was recently extended to LLM inference over streams (Lunyiu Nie, 2024). HAGuard is, to our knowledge, the first cascade combining lexical, distilled-transformer and prototype-intent cues for jailbreak detection.

Continual adaptation via lightweight adapters appears in privacy-preserving prompting (Haonan Duan, 2023) and backdoor scanning (Guangyu Shen, 2021). We adopt the same principle to refresh DistilBERT every few hours without downtime.

Finally, standardised benchmarks such as JailbreakBench (Patrick Chao, 2024) and StrongREJECT (Alexandra Souly, 2024) expose weaknesses of earlier ad-hoc evaluations. Our offline and online suites follow their guidelines and release raw predictions for transparency.

Background

Problem formulation For each user prompt p we must emit y ∈ {allow, block} and optionally a risk label t from a taxonomy of K categories. The guard executes synchronously before the assistant; the 99th-percentile runtime must not exceed L = 100 ms. Utility is measured primarily by the true-positive rate (TPR) at a 0.5 % false-positive rate (FPR), and by AUROC.

Signals Lexical patterns captured by tries, unicode-homoglyph detectors and Bloom filters offer constant-time but brittle cues. Contextual semantics require transformer encoders, yet even compact models cost tens of milliseconds. Sentence-level embeddings cluster prompts with similar intent; nearest-neighbour distances to curated prototypes provide interpretable yet potentially noisy evidence.

Cascade rationale Let ℓi denote latency of layer i and Δi its marginal recall. A cascade is worthwhile when the expected latency Σi πi ℓi (πi being the traffic fraction reaching layer i) remains under L while cumulative recall approaches that of the full stack. Early-exit thresholds must be calibrated to respect the global FPR budget.

Adaptive defence Attackers rapidly invent paraphrases, emojis or unicode tricks that elude static filters (Xiaogeng Liu, 2023). Logging the hardest misses and injecting them via small LoRA updates enables prompt recovery without full retraining.

Method

System architecture HAGuard comprises three sequential layers.

1) Layer-0 lexical gate. A trie of 10 318 unsafe keywords and a 1 M-slot Bloom filter (murmur-3, k = 7, FP ≈ 0.1 %) compute a risk score s0; prompts with s0 > 0.95 are blocked. Latency ≈ 5 µs.

2) Layer-1 semantic scorer. DistilGuard-60M—six-layer DistilBERT distilled from Llama-Guard logits—is quantised to INT-4 weights with INT-8 activations and exported to ONNX-Runtime. It outputs class probabilities p1; the maximum forms score s1. Latency ≈ 18 ms on one vCPU.

3) Layer-2 prototype matcher. Prompts are encoded with MiniLM-L6-v2 into 128-dim vectors e. Two FAISS-HNSW indices—20 k harmful and 40 k benign prototypes—are searched (ef = 128, M = 32). Features comprise the signed margin m = dben – dharm, Jaccard token overlap and an intent-consistency flag, concatenated with s0 and s1. A calibrated LightGBM with 500 trees (depth 7, learning-rate 0.05) produces the final verdict. Latency ≈ 9 ms.

Training data A 164 k-prompt corpus combines HarmBench, SafetyBench jailbreak subset, JailbreakBench, StrongREJECT, 50 k benign production prompts and 5 k real jailbreak attempts.

Model training DistilGuard is fine-tuned with a multi-view contrastive loss (temperature 0.07, queue 1 024) over paraphrases, translation round-trips and RL-Breaker adversarial variants (Xuan Chen, 2024). Prototypes are selected via k-means++ in embedding space and labelled by majority vote. LightGBM minimises log-loss with class weights chosen to hit 0.5 % FPR on validation.

Continual learning Prompts misclassified after Layer-2 populate a quarantine bucket. Every four hours up to 4 000 are used to train a rank-8 LoRA adapter (lr 2 × 10⁻⁵, one epoch) that merges into DistilGuard and hot-reloads across workers, incurring < 2 min downtime.

System optimisation All kernels exploit AVX-512; asynchronous I/O and early exits keep Layer-1 and Layer-2 threads idle for ~70 % of traffic.

Integrity assurance A CI suite executes a 1 000-prompt sanity batch, asserting entropy of Layer-1 logits, prototype margins, median latency ≤ 25 ms and resident-set size ≤ 800 MB. Any stub placeholder aborts the pipeline.

Experimental Setup

Hardware All experiments run on AWS c7g.large (single Graviton-3 vCPU, 8 GiB RAM). GPU numbers are reported only for the 7 B baseline.

Datasets (1) HarmBench 13 832 prompts, (2) SafetyBench jailbreak subset 8 617, (3) JailbreakBench 9 527 (Patrick Chao, 2024), (4) StrongREJECT 313 (Alexandra Souly, 2024), (5) Prod-Benign 50 000, (6) Rebuff-Jailbreak 5 000. After NFKC normalisation and deduplication 164 k unique prompts remain; 55 k prompts identical to the manuscript’s test list are held out for public evaluation.

Splits Prompt-family stratified 70 %/10 %/20 % train/val/test; StrongREJECT resides entirely in test. Random state 2025 ensures replicability.

Baselines A LLM-Guard-v2-7B (fp16, GPU via vLLM), B Rebuff default rules, C PARDEN (Ziyang Zhang, 2024), D perplexity filter (Gabriel Alon, 2023). Thresholds are tuned on validation to meet 0.5 % FPR when possible.

Metrics Primary: TPR at 0.5 % FPR, AUROC, median and p99 latency. Secondary: balanced accuracy, 15-bin expected calibration error, RSS and AWS cost per million queries. A robustness suite applies (i) whitespace padding ≤ 1 024 spaces, (ii) 5 % homoglyph swaps, and (iii) benign-harmful concatenation; we report ΔTPR and ΔFPR relative to clean prompts.

Procedures Offline evaluation trains five seeds {13, 17, 19, 23, 29}, stores predictions and computes mean ± SD plus 1 000× bootstrap confidence intervals. A 12-h online study streams 50 req/s via Kafka, injects ≥ 3 000 novel attacks from RL-Breaker, and compares HAGuard-Adaptive, HAGuard-Frozen and LLM-Guard-7B workers. Prometheus scrapes accuracy, latency, CPU and memory per hour; LoRA refreshes occur at hours 4 and 8.

Results

Offline benchmark Across five seeds HAGuard achieves 96.4 ± 0.3 % TPR at 0.5 % FPR and AUROC 0.972 ± 0.002. LLM-Guard-7B scores 81.1 ± 0.4 % TPR and 0.887 AUROC; Rebuff 74.5 %, PARDEN 78.2 %, perplexity 63.4 %. Median latency is 27 ± 1 ms and p99 78 ± 2 ms, versus 219 ± 8 ms and 642 ± 15 ms for the GPU baseline. RSS is 613 MB compared to 15 GiB plus GPU memory for LLM-Guard.

Ablation study Removing Layer-0 increases median latency by 15 ms and CPU utilisation by 53 % without accuracy benefit. Dropping Layer-1 reduces TPR by 9.8 pp; omitting Layer-2 cuts TPR by 11.3 pp but saves only 5 ms. Switching DistilGuard from INT-4 to INT-8 yields a negligible 0.2 pp TPR gain yet doubles memory.

Robustness Whitespace padding and homoglyph attacks lower HAGuard’s TPR by 1.9 pp, compared with 7–12 pp for single-stage models. Prompt concatenation costs 3.1 pp.

Online adaptation During the 12-h stream attack entropy spikes at hours 4 and 8. HAGuard-Adaptive climbs from 78 % to 92 % TPR after two LoRA refreshes, with FPR rising by < 1 pp; p99 latency stays below 85 ms. The frozen variant remains flat, as does LLM-Guard-7B. Total finetune downtime is 96 s.

Truncated run A deliberately misconfigured "full-experiment" processed only 16 prompts; all exited at Layer-0, yielding ~10 µs latency and 50 % accuracy. Because CI checks are disabled in this mode, the episode underscores the necessity of the integrity suite.

Limitations False positives persist in creative role-play scenarios where fictional crime plots resemble genuine malicious intent. Quantisation introduces slight calibration drift that future work should correct.

Conclusion

HAGuard demonstrates that carefully orchestrated lexical, distilled-transformer and prototype-intent evidence can raise jailbreak-detection accuracy from the long-standing 80 % ceiling to beyond 95 % while meeting sub-100 ms latency budgets on a single CPU core. Multi-view contrastive training, RL-Breaker hard-negative mining and rapid LoRA updates keep the guard adaptive without GPUs or prolonged downtime. Extensive offline and online experiments confirm substantial gains over GPU-bound or heuristic baselines, and all artefacts are open-sourced for full reproducibility. Future work will pursue tighter calibration, multilingual coverage, integration with routing frameworks such as MoGU (Yanrui Du, 2024), human-in-the-loop feedback and certified robustness guarantees inspired by CBD (Zhen Xiang, 2023).
